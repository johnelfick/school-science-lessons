5.1 Scientific names of Australian native foods
The term "Australian native foods" refers to Australian native plants known to be used by Australian aboriginal people for food.
Similarly, the term "bushfood", "bush foods", "bush tucker foods" refers to Australian native plants that Australians use or were known to use for food.
However, "bushfood" may refer to plants supplied by plant nurseries and labelled "bushfood", without evidence that they were Australian native foods.
Also, they may be hybrids subject to plant breeders' rights (PBR).
The Queensland Herbarium does not use the term "bush foods" in any publications and there is no agreed list of "bush foods".
However, in the list below, the common name preferred by the Austrlian National Herbarium or the Queensland Government is usually used.
Botanists avoid using the term "bush foods", because it refers to all of the indigenous foods for Australian indigenous peoples, including the protein component.
Botanists may prefer to use the term "Australian native foods" to differentiate between the foods the Aboriginal people used and the foods we daily use,
because we use their foods in a different way, and we use less of those foods available to them.
The term "Australian native foods", excludes any food that need processing to remove toxin, because their methods have been lost
The term "bushfoods" includes foods that need processing, and can be toxic.

5.4 Examples of uses of native plants
Orange Thorn (Citriobatus pauciflorus) has round yellow edible fruits with a leathery skin and large seeds.
Hop-bush, (Dodonaea viscosa), juice of the root was applied for toothache and cuts, and the chewed leaf and juice was put on stonefish and stingray stings and bound up for days.
Gymea Lily, (Doryanthes excelsa), flowering stems were cut and roasted, then made into a cake.
Ruby Saltbush, (Enchylaena tomentosa), tiny red flattened fruits were shaken off the bush and eaten.
Wombat Berry, (Eustrephus latifolius) roots were eaten by Aboriginal people.
Cherry Ballart, (Exocarpos cupressiformis), native cherry, wood was used for spearthrowers and for bull-roarers, and the sap was applied to snakebites.
Austral Mulberry, (Hedycarya angustifolia) straight sticks were used for fire-drills, twirled between the hands while resting on another flat piece of wood, to produce fire.
Austral Indigo, (Indigofera australis) and Wild Indigo, (Tephrosia purpurea) were crushed and put into pools to kill or stun fish so that they could be easily caught.
Mountain Devil, (Lambertia formosa) flowers were sucked for the copious nectar, which may have caused nausea and headache after consuming large amounts
Cabbage Palm, (Livistona australis), heart of the palm was eaten, but possibly not eaten before European settlement.
Spiny-headed Mat-rush, (Lomandra longifolia) long smooth leaves were used to make baskets and mats, and by beating and soaking the leaves, fibre was separated to make string for net-bags, baskets, and eel-traps.
Burrawangs, (Macrozamia spp. ), poisonous seeds of these and other cycads are borne in a large cone and have an orange outer coat.
Aboriginal people knew how to treat them to remove the poison, and so take advantage of the large amount of food provided by a single plant.
One of the ways was to cook the seed, break it up, and then soak it for up to three weeks in running water.
Nardoo fern , (Marsilea drummondii), which grows in shallow seasonal waters.
When the water dried up, the hard spore-cases were collected.
They were broken up on grindstones, and the spores were separated from the outer cases.
The spores swell when moistened, and were made into damper.
Murnong, (Microseris lanceolata), yam-daisy), radish-shaped tubers were cooked in baskets in an earth oven, to produce a dark sweet juice.
Antarctic Beech, (Nothofagus moorei), had an orange fungus, (Cyttaria septentrionalis), growin on this tree, which was eaten raw.
Geebung, (Persoonia pinifolia) had an edible the sweet pulp, and an inedible hard stone, which was discarded.
All Persoonia species are called gebung.
Common Reed, (Pragmites australis), had tall bamboo-like stems used spears, and were also cut into short lengths to make necklaces, or to stick through the septum of the nose as an ornament.
The leaves were used to make bags and baskets.
Brown Pine, (Podocarpus elatus), had sweet but mucilaginous seed borne on a purple-black fleshy stalk, which was eaten.
Thorny Saltbush, (Rhagodia spinescens), fruits were used to make a red paint for the face.
Kangaroo Apple, (Solanum laciniatum), egg-shaped orange fruits are poisonous if eaten unripe, so were eaten only when they were ripe enough to fall from the bush, or were picked earlier, and buried in sand-heaps to ripen.
Bluebell Creeper, (Sollya heterophylla), dark blue fruits were eaten.
Pepper Tree, (Tasmannia insipida), flesh of the purple-black fruit is edible, but the few black seeds are very peppery.
Cumbungi, (Typha spp), Bulrushes, underground rhizomes were steamed in an earth oven, the outer layer was stripped off, and the starchy fibrous inner part was tied in a knot to be chewed to remove the starch and leave the remaining fibre to be dried, soaked, scraped with mussel shells, and then rolled on the thigh to give very strong string for making large nets.
Finer string was made from the leaves.
The young shoots which appear in early summer were eaten raw.
